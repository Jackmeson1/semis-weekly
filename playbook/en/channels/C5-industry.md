# C5 Industry

Layer: L5 · Main horizon: far (six months or more)

## Explainer · Added

- **The demand test for semiconductors sits downstream**: a chip company's revenue ultimately depends on how much capex the hyperscalers are willing to spend. So to judge the direction of semiconductors, first look at how much customers are spending and how long they can afford to keep doing it.
- **TSMC monthly revenue**: published before the 10th of each month, it is the earliest first-hand reading on the whole AI chip chain, one to three months ahead of quarterly reports.
- **Monthly revenue of the Taiwan AI server chain**: assembly (Hon Hai, Quanta, Wistron, Wiwynn), power and cooling (Delta Electronics), and packaging and testing (ASE), which reflect the pace of server shipments.
- **Look at the change in the change**: if capex growth year over year falls from +60% to +30%, the absolute level is still rising but growth is slowing. Share prices often react first to a change in growth rate.

## What the author thinks

- Rule four, earnings are a test of the hyperscalers: the author holds that the issue is not earnings expectations (apart from TSLA and AAPL, the results this round will not look bad) but that the hyperscalers have to answer what return their capex is actually earning.
- INV-03: semiconductors are a purely supply-driven industry; once the big manufacturers decide to add capacity, the broad picture of strong demand and shortages does not change.
- Where hyperscaler capex comes from: the author argued that hyperscalers are not spending based on orders from Anthropic or OpenAI, but responding to demand from their own enterprise customers, which still exceeds supply.
- In 2026 the author repeatedly said memory is a commodity cycle and does not deserve a growth valuation; once memory price increases peak, gross margin peaks too.

## Common pitfalls

- Monthly revenue is in Taiwan dollars, so exchange rates affect the year-over-year figure.
- Not all of TSMC's revenue is AI; server-chain companies also have non-AI businesses.
- Not all hyperscaler capex goes to AI chips; some goes to land, buildings and networking. Finance leases are not counted in v1.
- This channel is slow. Do not over-read a single month's move; look for continuity.

## From the videos (the author, stated, 2024-05 to 2026-10, 83 videos)

What follows is the logic the author laid out in the weekly videos, recorded as they meant it. It does not mean the logic has been validated. The figures in brackets are the number of videos that support it and when. Where the author changed their position, the date is given. Items marked "added" are ours, not the author's. These are probability judgments, not laws. A conclusion is usually a chain of conditional judgments multiplied together; each link is likely but none is certain, and the author often says "most likely", "I think" or "fifty-fifty". Read each item as "under these conditions, it tends to go this way". When checking, look at which links have happened, one by one; do not test the whole conclusion as if it had to hold.

- **Read cloud capex as actual versus ever-higher expectations** (12 videos). In 2024 and early 2025 the consensus was only 15% and 20%, and the actual figures came in at 55% and 54%. So the watch level uses only actuals that have already been reported. About half of capex is buildings and land, depreciated over 20 years. The part spent on cards pays back in 1.5 to 2 years. A slowdown in growth therefore does not automatically mean chip demand is falling. The real turning point is when cloud revenue growth overtakes capex growth.
- **Rank shortage areas by how hard they are to expand** (7 videos). Prices rise first and capacity is added; once it is added, the market moves on to the next area. The market runs from the easy-to-expand end to the hard-to-expand end. The gap between expectations and reality is bigger for GPUs and CPUs than for memory, and smallest for optical modules. Areas that are easy to expand and have already risen (general-purpose NAND, optical modules) are bought only after a sharp drop.
- **The bottleneck moves** (2026-04 to 2026-09). In 2026-04 the author said power was not short and that construction was ahead of the supply of cards. From 2026-09 the author changed this: approvals and the grid became the constraint, and for empty data halls waiting for cards the bottleneck shifted from chips to power.
