# C7 Policy and events

Layer: L1.fed_path, L1.fiscal_politics, L0.geopolitics · Main horizon: mid

## Explainer · Added

- This channel holds things that cannot be expressed as a single number: FOMC meetings, tariffs, elections, wars and export controls.
- **In the author's ranking the Fed rate-cut path is always last** (HZ-05, net −53). Its effect on assets has to pass through long-end rates and liquidity before it shows up, so reading C2 directly is enough.
- **A policy shock's veto needs an expiry date** (CAL-01). When a shock first arrives everything else gives way; once prices stop reacting to new policy news, the veto should end, without waiting for the policy itself to take effect.

## What the author thinks

- The number of cuts does not matter: the author holds that the rate-cut topic is stale and the market need not focus on it, and that two cuts versus one makes little difference; it does not help long-end rates and cannot produce a liquidity-driven bull market.
- SW-M-03: when the market prices in too many cuts, the importance of macro returns.
- CAL-01, the author's own assessment of their biggest mistake: after leaving the market at the end of the previous year, the biggest error of that year was not daring to buy the dip in April, having failed to see that the tariff threats would ultimately be withdrawn.

## Signals

There is no automatic signal in v1. The inputs are:
- The calendar: FOMC meetings, major data releases, known policy milestones (`sources/calendar.yaml`)
- You can paste the news headlines you are watching this week into `inputs/<week>/notes.md`; the model judges only from what you provide and does not look for news itself

## Common pitfalls

- Do not write a separate conclusion about rate-cut expectations; leave that to C2.
- During a policy shock, record the date the shock began, and each week check whether prices are still reacting to new news, to judge whether the veto has expired.

## From the videos (the author, stated, 2024-05 to 2026-10, 83 videos)

What follows is the logic the author laid out in the weekly videos, recorded as they meant it. It does not mean the logic has been validated. The figures in brackets are the number of videos that support it and when. Where the author changed their position, the date is given. Items marked "added" are ours, not the author's. These are probability judgments, not laws. A conclusion is usually a chain of conditional judgments multiplied together; each link is likely but none is certain, and the author often says "most likely", "I think" or "fifty-fifty". Read each item as "under these conditions, it tends to go this way". When checking, look at which links have happened, one by one; do not test the whole conclusion as if it had to hold.

- **Expiry is judged by prices and actual policy, not headlines** (about 12 videos, consistent from 2025-04 to 2026-03). There are two checks. When the same kind of bad news comes out, does the price still fall? Has the policy actually in force (the effective tax rate, or whether there are substitute provisions) changed? A withdrawal, a truce or a successful negotiation does not count as expiry by itself. Neither does one law being blocked while other laws can still be used to add the measure.
- **Two layers** (7 videos). The sentiment and price layer expires in days to weeks. The real-economy layer (prices, jobs, earnings) shows up only about three to six months after the policy takes effect. After the price layer expires, the log still marks "real-economy drag remains."
- **Set the expected expiry by type** (7 videos). Tariffs used to pressure a negotiation expire quickly. Broad tariffs that change trade structure expire slowly. For events that are "waiting for a result," such as elections and government shutdowns, the shock usually expires once the result lands and uncertainty is removed, and most of the shock comes beforehand. A hot war ends only when the other side agrees to a ceasefire, so the tariff idea of "expiry means a rebound" does not apply.
- **A threat is not a shock; an effective date, legislation or a ruling is** (8 videos). By default only the latter triggers a veto, unless prices have already started to fall. In 2025-01 the author thought most threats were loud thunder and little rain. On 2025-04-01 this changed to "may be worse than expected." From 2025-05 it went back to "most will be withdrawn, but watch for the effective date."
- **What to do during a shock** (2025). While a policy shock is taking effect and has not yet expired, leave existing positions alone, do not bet on the outcome of negotiations, and do not switch direction because of a rebound.
- **The effective tariff rate is a reading of the size of the shock.** The author's rough estimate is that each 1 percentage point increase adds about 0.1 to inflation and cuts about 0.1 from growth. Record the level, but do not treat it as a threshold.
- **For geopolitical oil, look at duration, not the peak** (consistent in two episodes, 2025-06 and 2026-03). A shipping disruption of a few weeks and one of a few months are two different shocks. The point at which half of global commercial inventories have been used up is when demand is forced to be cut and prices spike in panic. Most of the oil price rise comes during the build-up of tension. If the price stops rising after the outbreak, the shock has expired. News of negotiations does not count as relief; wait for traffic to resume.
- **Government shutdown** (4 videos). When the odds of a shutdown rise, hedge funds cut leverage first. During a shutdown, data stops being released and growth expectations cool, and the long end often falls. It usually expires when the government reopens. (Note: Treasury auctions go on as usual during a shutdown; what restricts issuance is the debt ceiling.)
- **Policy support** (2025-03, changed 2025-05). In 2025-03 the author thought there was no support, at least for that year. From 2025-05 the author changed to saying the White House cares about the market. Support appearing can help judge expiry, but it is not automatically a buy point.
